On the Number of Holes in a Common Drinking Straw

A multi-theoretic census
M. R. Annulus1
Department of Beverage Topology
Institute for Applied Pedantry
16 May 2026
Abstract We settle, with what we concede is gratuitous machinery, the recurring popular question of how many holes are possessed by a standard drinking straw. The straw is homotopy equivalent to the circle; we therefore compute its homotopy groups, singular and de Rham cohomology rings, group (co)homology, complex and real K-theory, oriented bordism, stable homotopy, and an Alexander-dual linking invariant. Every invariant designed to count holes returns the same value, namely $1$. The two boundary circles occasionally mistaken for additional holes are demonstrated to be boundary, not topology.
Keywords. drinking straw · homotopy type · Bott periodicity · Alexander duality · beverage cylinder · Möbius distinction.
MSC 2020. 55-02, 55N15, 55N20, 55Q10, 57R75.

§ 1. Introduction

It is a recurring scandal of online life that the question how many holes does a drinking straw have? excites passionate disagreement. The proposed answers are typically one, two, or, in moments of metaphysical zeal, zero. We resolve the matter using essentially every tool a graduate algebraic topology course leaves lying around.

The strategy is elementary. A straw is homeomorphic to the cylinder $S^1\times[0,1]$, which deformation retracts onto its core circle $S^1$. Every homotopy-invariant feature of the straw therefore agrees with that of $S^1$. We compute these invariants in a representative selection of (co)homology theories and confirm that they unanimously detect one hole.

§ 2. The space and its homotopy type

Definition 2.1 (Straw). A straw is a topological space $X$ homeomorphic to $S^1\times[0,1]$. The boundary circles $S^1\times\{0\}$ and $S^1\times\{1\}$ are called the mouth and the foot; the open subset $S^1\times(0,1)$ is the bore.

The projection $S^1\times[0,1]\to S^1$ is a deformation retraction, so $X\simeq S^1$. This single fact will do almost all of the work below.

§ 3. Homotopy groups

The circle is the prototypical Eilenberg–MacLane space $K(\mathbb{Z},1)$: $$\pi_1(X)\;=\;\mathbb{Z},\qquad \pi_n(X)\;=\;0\quad(n\ne 1).$$ The generator of $\pi_1$ is the loop that winds once through the bore. There is a $\mathbb{Z}$-worth of homotopy classes of loops, parametrised by winding number, and only one hole through which to wind them.

§ 4. Singular homology

With integer coefficients, $$H_0(X;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z},\qquad H_1(X;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z},\qquad H_n(X;\mathbb{Z})=0\;(n\geq 2).$$ The Betti numbers are $(b_0,b_1)=(1,1)$, the Euler characteristic vanishes, and the Poincaré polynomial is $P_X(t)=1+t$. The lone $\mathbb{Z}$ in degree one is the hole.

Remark 4.2. The mouth and foot cobound the cylinder and are therefore homologous in $X$. They contribute jointly to the single generator of $H_1$, not separately. This is the principal source of the popular two-hole heresy.

§ 5. Cohomology ring

As a graded ring, $$H^*(X;\mathbb{Z})\;\cong\;\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\big/(\alpha^2),\qquad |\alpha|=1,$$ the exterior algebra on a single one-dimensional generator. There is nothing for $\alpha$ to cup into in higher degree, and the ring structure offers no information beyond the additive one.

§ 6. de Rham cohomology

Working on the smooth open straw $S^1\times\mathbb{R}$, $$H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)=\mathbb{R},\qquad H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)=\mathbb{R}\cdot[d\theta],$$ where $d\theta$ is the angular one-form: closed, globally non-exact, with $\oint_\gamma d\theta=2\pi$. The form $d\theta$ is, quite literally, the differential form that knows about the hole. Stokes will object if one tries to write it as $df$.

§ 7. Group (co)homology

The circle is the classifying space $B\mathbb{Z}$, hence $$H_*(X;\mathbb{Z})\;=\;H_*(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{Z}),\qquad H^*(X;\mathbb{Z})\;=\;H^*(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{Z}).$$ In some sense the hole-count of a straw is an invariant of the integers as a group — a tautology of pleasing depth.

§ 8. Complex K-theory

Every complex vector bundle on $S^1$ is trivialisable, since $\pi_0\,U(n)=0$ for all $n$. Hence $$K^0(X)\;=\;\mathbb{Z},\qquad \widetilde K^{\,0}(X)\;=\;0,\qquad K^1(X)\;=\;\mathbb{Z},$$ with the odd part generated by the clutching function $z\mapsto z$. Complex K-theory sees the hole in degree one and nothing else.

§ 9. Real K-theory; or, the Möbius distinction

Real K-theory is more discerning. Bott periodicity gives $$\widetilde{KO}^{\,0}(X)\;=\;\mathbb{Z}/2,$$ generated by the Möbius line bundle. Ordinary homology and complex K-theory cannot distinguish a straw from a Möbius strip — they share homotopy type — but $KO$-theory can. The straw represents the trivial class; the Möbius strip, the nontrivial one. Real K-theory therefore certifies that the cylinder under consideration is, in fact, a straw and not a twisted impostor.2

§ 10. Oriented bordism

The coefficients vanish, $\Omega_1^{SO}(\mathrm{pt})=0$, but the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence yields $$\Omega_1^{SO}(X)\;=\;\mathbb{Z},$$ generated by the inclusion of the core circle. The core represents a nontrivial $1$-dimensional oriented bordism class over $X$.

§ 11. Stable homotopy

For the universal generalised cohomology theory we have $$\widetilde\pi_n^{\,s}(X)\;\cong\;\pi_{n-1}^{\,s},$$ so the stable invariants of a straw are a degree shift of the stable stems: $$\mathbb{Z},\;\mathbb{Z}/2,\;\mathbb{Z}/2,\;\mathbb{Z}/24,\;0,\;0,\;\mathbb{Z}/2,\;\mathbb{Z}/240,\;\ldots$$ Most of these classes have nothing whatsoever to do with the hole; they are facts about the geometry of exotic spheres which, by an accident of stable algebra, also pertain to the straw.

§ 12. Alexander duality, and why the hands agree

Embed the core $S^1\hookrightarrow S^3$. Alexander duality gives $$\widetilde H_1\!\big(S^3\setminus S^1\big)\;\cong\;\widetilde H^{\,1}(S^1)\;=\;\mathbb{Z},$$ and the linking pairing $$H_1(X)\;\otimes\;H_1(S^3\!\setminus\!X)\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb{Z}$$ is the identity. Translation: there is exactly one independent way to thread a closed loop through the straw, and its threading is detected by linking number. This is the invariant one computes physically by passing a piece of string through the bore.

§ 13. Conclusion

Theorem 13.1 (Main Theorem). Every (co)homological invariant designed to count holes returns the same value when evaluated on a drinking straw, namely $\;1\;$.

The two-hole interpretation conflates boundary with topology: the straw has two boundary circles, but they cobound the cylinder and represent a single class in $H_1$. The zero-hole interpretation conflates contractibility with embeddedness in $\mathbb{R}^3$, an error we do not dignify further. We rest.
